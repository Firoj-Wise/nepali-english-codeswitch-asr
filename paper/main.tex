\section{Introduction}
Code-switching, the alternation between two or more languages within a single conversational turn, is ubiquitous across South Asia. In spoken Nepali, speakers frequently embed English lexical items, technical vocabulary, and colloquial phrases within a Devanagari grammatical matrix.

Despite the recent scaling of multilingual speech foundation models~\cite{canary2025,indic_transcribe_flex}, code-switched ASR systems consistently suffer from two structural error modes:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Script confusion:} transcribing English loanwords into Devanagari phonetics (e.g., rendering English ``doctor'' phonetically in Devanagari) or erroneously transliterating native Nepali words into Latin characters.
  \item \textbf{Switch-point brittleness:} acoustic and prosodic shifts occurring at the transition between Devanagari and Latin orthographies cause disproportionate insertion, substitution, and deletion rates immediately surrounding switch boundaries.
\end{enumerate}

Traditional approaches attempt to resolve script confusion through auxiliary token-level language identification or diarization heads~\cite{token_diarization2022}. However, hard token-level classification objectives during autoregressive generation often introduce excessive stiffness at boundary tokens, frequently inducing mis-transliterations when acoustic ambiguity arises.

Meanwhile, sequence-level policy optimization such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) has recently emerged as an effective framework for speech alignment~\cite{shao2024deepseekmath,amazon_grpo2025}. Kumar et al.~\cite{synthetic_grpo2026} demonstrated that GRPO on synthetic speech dramatically reduces insertion errors for decoder-only speech LLMs, while Ye and Vickers~\cite{ye_vickers2026} showed that GRPO with a joint error-rate and script-fidelity reward reduces cross-script contamination on synthetic audio across ten language pairs. However, both prior efforts evaluated decoder-only audio language models (e.g., Qwen2-Audio) on read or synthetic speech. Whether synthetic-to-real policy optimization transfers to dedicated, high-efficiency encoder--decoder foundation architectures on unconstrained, spontaneous conversational code-switched speech remains an open empirical question.

In this work, our primary contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Synthetic-to-real transfer in encoder--decoder ASR:} we demonstrate that a dedicated FastConformer encoder--decoder model fine-tuned exclusively on 0.95\,h of synthetic code-switched audio effectively transfers zero-shot to unconstrained human conversational speech.
  \item \textbf{Empirical analysis of script-aware GRPO:} we formulate a joint sequence reward combining MER and SCR. We evaluate the boundary dynamics of sequence-level RL relative to token-level inference gating on real development speech.
  \item \textbf{Open benchmark and rigorous evaluation:} we release 16.35\,h (13,044 utterances; 10,214 test, 2,830 dev) of verified Nepali--English podcast audio on Hugging Face, validating all experimental claims with paired bootstrap confidence intervals (1,000 resamples) and monolingual retention checks.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Code-switched speech recognition.}
Early investigations into South Asian code-switching focused on hybrid acoustic models and balanced monolingual pre-training~\cite{mucs2021,bhasa_indic2021}. Modern foundation architectures such as IndicTranscribeFlex~\cite{indic_transcribe_flex} and Canary~\cite{canary2025} incorporate massive multilingual vocabularies, yet remain susceptible to script flipping when alternating between non-Latin Indic orthographies and English.

\paragraph{Language identification and token gating.}
Prior work addressed script ambiguity by attaching auxiliary frame- or token-level language identification heads~\cite{token_diarization2022}. While effective on scripted speech, hard token-level constraints often force premature orthographic commitments that degrade spontaneous speech recognition.

\paragraph{Reinforcement learning in speech.}
Policy-gradient and minimum word error rate (MWER) training have an extensive history in ASR~\cite{prabhavalkar2018mwer}. GRPO~\cite{shao2024deepseekmath,amazon_grpo2025} eliminates the need for a separate critic network by estimating advantages from sampled candidate groups. Furthermore, training speech recognition models on synthetic acoustic data has proven effective for low-resource domains~\cite{rossenbach2020generating,fazel2021synthasr,synthetic_grpo2026}. Ye and Vickers~\cite{ye_vickers2026} demonstrated that script-aware rewards curb script confusion in audio LLMs. We extend this inquiry to investigate whether sequence-level rewards provide complementary boundary regularization when adapting a pre-trained encoder--decoder model to spontaneous conversational speech.

\section{Dataset Construction}

\subsection{Synthetic Training Corpus}
To eliminate the requirement for expensive human recordings during training, we synthesized a controlled code-switched corpus. Sentences containing natural intra-sentential Nepali--English code-switching were generated by prompting Gemini 1.5 Pro with conversational templates reflecting modern urban discourse. All sentences were filtered against strict lexical criteria and a 5-gram non-leakage check against the real benchmark references.

Audio was synthesized utilizing two complementary acoustic engines:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{OmniVoice~\cite{zhu2026omnivoice}:} an open-source diffusion acoustic model fine-tuned on 100+ hours of high-quality native Nepali speech across varied speaker voices. While base OmniVoice nominally supports Nepali, its zero-shot synthesis exhibits pronounced Indian accentuation rather than authentic Nepali cadence; domain fine-tuning aligns the model with native prosody and fluid code-mixed transitions.
  \item \textbf{Gemini TTS:} multi-speaker neural synthesis (\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash}) providing diverse English accentuation across four distinct vocal timbres.
\end{itemize}
We pair these engines because neither system alone satisfies the dual acoustic demands of code-switched speech. Commercial multilingual engines produce natural English intonation and diverse timbres, but often distort native Nepali retroflex consonants and schwa deletion. Conversely, the fine-tuned OmniVoice captures authentic Devanagari phonology and colloquial code-mix phrasing, but lacks multi-accent English variety. Combining both provides rich phonetic coverage across both languages and prevents the ASR encoder from overfitting to artifacts of any single vocoder.
Each candidate text template was synthesized across multiple voices ($N \ge 2$) and acoustic engines. From 258 candidate code-switched text templates, all audio was sampled at 16\,kHz mono, augmented with telephony bandpass filtering (300--3400\,Hz) and 10--30\,dB SNR noise to simulate channel variability and bridge the domain gap to real-world speech. Utterances were screened through an independent round-trip filter (\texttt{whisper-large-v3-turbo} in Devanagari-only scope), pruning clips with CER $> 0.20$. The resulting verified synthetic training split comprises 517 audio utterances across 211 unique sentences (0.95\,h total duration).

\subsection{Real Spontaneous Benchmark}
To rigorously assess generalization, we curated a spontaneous conversational benchmark from long-form public Nepali podcasts across 28 diverse episodes. Unlike read speech, podcast audio contains spontaneous turn-taking, ambient noise, conversational laughter, and natural speech rate variations.

Transcripts adhere to a strict orthographic convention: native Nepali matrix words are transcribed in Devanagari, English words and embedded phrases are transcribed in Latin script as spoken (not transliterated), and attached Nepali postpositions are separated into distinct Devanagari tokens (e.g., Latin root ``\textit{phone}'' followed by separated Devanagari postposition ``\textit{-ma}'', rather than fused into a single compound).

\paragraph{Dataset verification protocol.}
Every clip in the initial 13,050-utterance podcast corpus was individually audited against raw 16\,kHz PCM audio. The verification pipeline: (i) identified and quarantined 233 clips exhibiting acoustic background loops; (ii) eliminated 161 machine translation leakages where spoken Nepali words were mistakenly transcribed as English translations, restoring original Devanagari; (iii) corrected 819 artificial token-doubling glitches caused by diarization timestamp overlaps; and (iv) normalized 86 digit instances into verbalized spoken words. The remaining 13,044 clean clips constitute the verified benchmark.

\paragraph{Data statement and ethics.}
The benchmark audio originates from public Nepali conversational podcasts distributed under open broadcast terms and CC~BY~4.0. Speakers are adult conversationalists discussing socio-technical, educational, and entrepreneurial topics in urban Kathmandu colloquial speech. The corpus is partitioned into:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Real Dev Split:} 2,830 utterances (3.55\,h, 16 episodes, 20 speakers).
  \item \textbf{Frozen Real Test Split:} 10,214 utterances (12.80\,h, 25 episodes, 52 speakers).
\end{itemize}
Crucially, dev and test splits are strictly speaker-disjoint (zero overlapping speakers). Test audio was frozen with SHA-256 integrity verification prior to model evaluation. The complete 16.35\,h benchmark is available on Hugging Face under CC~BY~4.0 at \url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/SamaFiroz/nepali-english-codeswitch-podcasts}.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Base Architecture and Adaptation}
We adopt \texttt{indic-transcribe-flex}~\cite{indic_transcribe_flex}, a 1.2-billion parameter multilingual model built on the Canary-1B-v2 architecture comprising a frozen FastConformer acoustic encoder and an autoregressive Transformer decoder with a 7,152-token SentencePiece aggregate vocabulary. Given an acoustic feature sequence $X$, the encoder produces representations $H = \text{Encoder}(X) \in \mathbb{R}^{T' \times D}$. 
During adaptation, the encoder parameters remain completely frozen to preserve general acoustic representations. We apply Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)~\cite{hu2022lora} to the decoder query ($W_q$) and value ($W_v$) projection layers:
\begin{equation}
  W = W_0 + \frac{\alpha_{\text{lora}}}{r} B A,
\end{equation}
where $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ denotes the frozen base weights, and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ are trainable rank-$r$ matrices ($r = 16, \alpha_{\text{lora}} = 32$).

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Code-switched evaluation requires decoupled assessment of lexical accuracy, orthographic consistency, and boundary stability:

\paragraph{Mixed Error Rate (MER).}
Evaluates Word Error Rate (WER) on Latin-script segments and Character Error Rate (CER) on Devanagari segments:
\begin{equation}
  \text{MER} = \frac{E_{\text{w,en}} + E_{\text{c,ne}}}{N_{\text{w,en}} + N_{\text{c,ne}}},
\end{equation}
where $E_{\text{w,en}} = S_{\text{en}} + D_{\text{en}} + I_{\text{en}}$ is the word edit distance over English segments, $E_{\text{c,ne}}$ is the character edit distance over Devanagari segments, and $N$ denotes reference token count.

\paragraph{Script Confusion Rate (SCR).}
Let $\mathcal{M}$ be the set of aligned character pairs between hypothesis $y$ and reference $y^*$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \text{SCR} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|} \sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{M}} \mathbb{I}\bigl[\text{script}(y_i) \neq \text{script}(y^*_j)\bigr].
\end{equation}
$\SCRen$ specifically restricts $\mathcal{M}$ to Latin-script reference characters, measuring English loanwords erroneously transcribed into Devanagari.

\paragraph{Switch-Point Error Rate (SPER).}
The MER computed strictly over reference tokens within a local context window of $w = \pm 1$ tokens around language switch points.

\subsection{Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)}
In Stage 1, the model is adapted on synthetic pairs $(x, y)$ using cross-entropy loss over target token sequences:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t}, x).
\end{equation}
We optimize with AdamW ($\text{lr} = 1\times 10^{-5}$, effective batch size 8, 1,000 steps).

\subsection{Baseline: Token-Level Script Gating (\texorpdfstring{\sys{2}}{S2})}
As an architectural comparison, an auxiliary script classification head is attached atop the decoder hidden states $h_t$:
\begin{equation}
  P_{\text{gate}}(s_t \mid h_t) = \text{sigmoid}(W_g h_t + b_g),
\end{equation}
where $s_t \in \{0, 1\}$ denotes Devanagari versus Latin script. Training minimizes $\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}} + \lambda_g \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}$ with $\lambda_g = 0.1$. At inference, logits for candidate tokens matching the predicted script are biased by $\gamma \log P_{\text{gate}}$ ($\gamma = 2.0$, applied when $P_{\text{gate}} > 0.9$).

\subsection{Stage 2: Script-Aware Policy Optimization (GRPO)}
While SFT aligns the decoder with code-switched acoustics, cross-entropy penalizes script confusion equally with ordinary lexical substitutions. To directly incentivize orthographic fidelity at language boundaries, we implement Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)~\cite{shao2024deepseekmath,amazon_grpo2025} trained on the 517 synthetic utterances.

For each acoustic sequence $x$, the policy $\pi_\theta$ samples a group of $G = 8$ candidate transcriptions $\{y^{(1)}, \dots, y^{(G)}\}$ at temperature $\tau = 1.0$. A scalar reward $R(y^{(g)}, y^*)$ is computed against ground truth $y^*$. Group advantages are normalized:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{A}^{(g)} = \frac{R(y^{(g)}, y^*) - \mu_G}{\sigma_G + \delta},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_G$ and $\sigma_G$ are the mean and standard deviation of rewards within the sampled group, and $\delta = 10^{-8}$ prevents division by zero. The GRPO objective maximizes:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:grpo}
\begin{aligned}
  \mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\Bigg[ 
    &\frac{1}{G} \sum_{g=1}^{G} \sum_{t=1}^{|y^{(g)}|} 
    \min \Big( r_t^{(g)}(\theta) \hat{A}^{(g)}, \\
    &\quad \clip\bigl(r_t^{(g)}(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon\bigr) \hat{A}^{(g)} \Big) \\
    &\quad - \beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \parallel \pi_{\text{ref}}) \Bigg],
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $r_t^{(g)}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t^{(g)} \mid y_{<t}^{(g)}, x)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_t^{(g)} \mid y_{<t}^{(g)}, x)}$ denotes the per-token importance sampling ratio at decoding step $t$. Here, $\pi_\theta$ represents the active autoregressive decoder policy parameterized by LoRA weights $\theta$, and $\pi_{\text{old}}$ is the policy snapshot used to generate candidate rollouts. Because the policy is initialized directly from the converged Stage~1 SFT checkpoint and constrained via low-rank parameter updates (LoRA, $r=16$), we set $\beta = 0.0$, relying on group advantage normalization and PPO-style clipping ($\epsilon = 0.2$) to govern update step sizes without an explicit reference policy penalty. Each candidate hypothesis $y^{(g)} = (y_1^{(g)}, \dots, y_{|y^{(g)}|}^{(g)})$ consists of tokens generated autoregressively conditioned on past tokens $y_{<t}^{(g)}$ and acoustic representations $x$. The clipping hyperparameter $\epsilon = 0.2$ bounds the gradient updates to maintain training stability.

\paragraph{Reward formulation.}
Our joint script-aware reward combines sequence accuracy with orthographic fidelity:
\begin{equation}
  R_{\text{joint}}(y, y^*) = \bigl(1 - \text{MER}(y, y^*)\bigr) + \lambda_{\text{scr}} \bigl(1 - \text{SCR}(y, y^*)\bigr),
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{\text{scr}} = 0.5$. Note that setting $\lambda_{\text{scr}} = 0.0$ corresponds to the MER-only reward ablation ($S_3$).

\subsection{Statistical Significance}
To assess whether improvements are statistically reliable, we executed paired bootstrap hypothesis tests with 1,000 resamples ($B=1{,}000$, seed 42) on the frozen test split:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\sys{4} vs.\ \sys{0}:} achieves an absolute MER reduction of $-1.90\pp$ (95\% CI: $[-2.37, -1.42]$), a SPER reduction of $-7.19\pp$ (95\% CI: $[-7.97, -6.46]$), and an English script confusion reduction of $-0.35\pp$ (95\% CI: $[-0.65, -0.04]$). All intervals strictly exclude zero ($p < 0.05$).
  \item \textbf{\sys{4} vs.\ \sys{1}:} demonstrates that sequence-level policy optimization provides statistically significant fine-grained gains in boundary fidelity ($\Delta \text{SPER} = -0.07\pp$, 95\% CI: $[-0.12, -0.03]$, $p < 0.05$) and English script confusion ($\Delta \SCRen = -0.09\pp$, 95\% CI: $[-0.13, -0.06]$, $p < 0.05$). Paired bootstrap estimation for MER yields a mean difference of $-0.03\pp$ (95\% CI: $[-0.05, -0.01]$, $p < 0.05$).
\end{itemize}
To guard against potential intra-episode correlation across conversational sessions, we additionally conducted an episode-clustered block bootstrap ($B=1{,}000$ resamples clustered across the 25 test podcast episodes). Clustered analysis confirms that the fine-grained advantage of \sys{4} over \sys{1} remains statistically significant: $\Delta \text{SPER} = -0.07\pp$ (95\% CI: $[-0.15, -0.01]$, $p = 0.022$), $\Delta \SCRen = -0.09\pp$ (95\% CI: $[-0.11, -0.06]$, $p < 0.001$), and $\Delta \text{MER} = -0.03\pp$ (95\% CI: $[-0.05, -0.01]$, $p = 0.008$).

Comparing \sys{3} ($\lambda_{\text{scr}} = 0.0$) against \sys{4} ($\lambda_{\text{scr}} = 0.5$), both attain equivalent performance ($18.89\%$ MER, $25.53\%$ SPER, $5.24\%$ $\SCRen$). This confirms that the benefit of policy optimization on encoder--decoder models stems from sequence-level boundary exploration rather than aggressive script re-weighting alone.

The domain adaptation suppresses erroneous Latin alphabet insertions that previously afflicted native Devanagari transcriptions, reducing SCR from $8.71\%$ to $6.95\%$.

\paragraph{English (LibriSpeech test-clean~\cite{librispeech2015}, 1,000 utterances, 1.50\,h).}
On monolingual English, lexical accuracy is statistically preserved across adaptation: WER shifts from $3.33\%$ (\sys{0}) to $3.42\%$ (\sys{1}) and recovers to $3.38\%$ under \sys{4} (95\% CI vs.\ \sys{0}: $[-0.17, +0.30]$, $p > 0.05$). MER increases from $4.21\%$ to $5.27\%$ and SCR from $0.59\%$ to $1.03\%$, reflecting occasional Devanagari character insertions on pure English speech induced by the code-switched prior. S1 and S4 exhibit nearly identical profiles, confirming that English orthographic trade-offs stem from SFT adaptation rather than the GRPO objective.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis and Error Inspection}
Detailed inspection of transcriptions on the frozen test split illustrates how script-aware GRPO resolves boundary confusion:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Suppression of spurious transliteration:} In baseline model \sys{0}, common English words such as ``someone'' and ``brands'' are frequently transcribed into phonetic Devanagari characters or split into mixed-script fragments (e.g., generating Devanagari roots with appended Latin plural suffixes). Under \sys{4}, the sequence-level reward penalizes cross-script character boundaries, restoring continuous English orthography.
  \item \textbf{Stability at language transitions:} Transition regions between native Nepali matrix verbs and embedded English noun phrases regularly induce insertion hallucinations in unadapted models. Synthetic SFT provides strong acoustic anchoring for code-switched prosody, while GRPO optimizes group-level sequence preferences, reducing boundary switch-point errors.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}
While synthetic SFT combined with GRPO produces statistically reliable gains, four practical limitations remain:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Modest RL gains relative to prior LLM studies:} While Kumar et al.~\cite{synthetic_grpo2026} observed dramatic relative error reductions ($>40\%$) from GRPO on synthetic data with decoder-only speech LLMs, our post-SFT GRPO gains on real spontaneous speech are more modest ($-0.07\pp$ SPER, $-0.09\pp$ $\SCRen$, $-0.03\pp$ MER). We attribute this to: (i) structural architecture, where our FastConformer encoder--decoder model is already constrained by joint acoustic representations and SFT convergence, leaving less headroom than unanchored LLM decoders that suffer from massive insertion hallucinations; (ii) data overlap, as optimizing GRPO on the same 517 synthetic utterances previously fitted during SFT yields low reward variance across rollout candidate groups; and (iii) the acoustic domain gap between clean synthetic audio and spontaneous conversational podcasts.
  \item \textbf{Small synthetic training volume:} Training relies on 517 utterances (0.95\,h), and Nepali speech is synthesized across two male timbres.
  \item \textbf{English orthographic side-effects:} MER and SCR slightly increase on monolingual English despite unchanged WER.
  \item \textbf{Demographic scope:} The podcast benchmark predominantly reflects urban conversational discourse; regional rural dialects require further empirical validation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
We have presented an empirical study demonstrating that supervised fine-tuning on purely synthetic code-switched audio provides the primary transfer to spontaneous human conversational speech ($-1.88\pp$ MER, $-7.12\pp$ SPER). Sequence-level reinforcement learning using Script-Aware GRPO provides complementary fine-grained boundary regularization, outperforming token-level inference gating on dev and yielding small but statistically significant reductions in switch-point error rate ($-0.07\pp$) and English script confusion ($-0.09\pp$, $p < 0.05$) without degrading English lexical recognition accuracy (WER preserved at 3.38\% vs.\ 3.33\%), while improving native Nepali MER by 2.38\pp{}.

Code and configurations are open-sourced at \url{https://github.com/Firoj-Wise/nepali-english-codeswitch-asr}. The complete conversational benchmark (13,044 utterances, 16.35\,h) is available on the Hugging Face Hub at \url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/SamaFiroz/nepali-english-codeswitch-podcasts}.
